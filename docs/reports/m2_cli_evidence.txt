# radius + mean density (1 km sphere of water)
$ ./target/debug/blackhole calc --radius 1 --radius-unit km --density water --label 1 km water sphere
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Results
-------
  Object                                 1 km water sphere
  Mass                                   4.188790e12 kg (= 4.188790e9 t)
  Physical radius R                      1.000000e3 m (= 1.000000e0 km)
  Mean density (sphere model)            1.000000e3 kg/m3 (= 1.000000e0 g/cm3)  [water (model assumption: Nominal 1000 kg/m3; liquid water at 4 degC is 999.97 kg/m3)]
  Schwarzschild radius r_s               6.221325e-15 m
  Compactness ratio r_s / R              6.221325e-18
  Ratio R / r_s                          1.607375e17
  Critical radius at assumed density     4.009208e11 m (= 2.679990e0 au)

Interpretation
--------------
  1. Schwarzschild comparison. r_s = 6.221325e-15 m and R = 1.000000e3 m (=
  1.000000e0 km), so r_s / R = 6.221325e-18 and R / r_s = 1.607375e17. Under
  the stated assumptions the surface lies outside the horizon. This compares
  two lengths of the model; it is not a prediction about collapse.
  2. Critical radius. At the assumed mean density of 1.000000e3 kg/m3 (=
  1.000000e0 g/cm3), a homogeneous sphere reaches r_s = R at R_krit =
  4.009208e11 m (= 2.679990e0 au). The object's radius is R / R_krit =
  2.494259e-9. This object's radius is therefore smaller than the critical
  radius, so the assumed density is far too low for it to be its own
  horizon. The ratio follows the assumption and scales as rho^(-1/2).

Important limitations
---------------------
  1. Every mass has a Schwarzschild radius r_s = 2 G M / c^2, so there is no
     universal minimum mass for a black hole. A mass and an r_s say nothing on
     their own about whether a horizon exists.

  2. The density is a model assumption. Compressing a real body changes its
     density, so the critical radius moves with the assumption; it is not a
     fixed property of the object.

  3. Comparing a radius with a Schwarzschild radius compares two lengths. It is
     not a simulation of gravitational collapse.

  4. Whether a horizon actually forms depends on the spacetime geometry, on the
     state of the matter (pressure, temperature, composition, angular momentum)
     and on the dynamics of the collapse. None of that is modelled here.

  5. Newtonian mechanics cannot represent an event horizon. N-body dynamics,
     relativistic ray tracing and gravitational lensing are separate, later
     milestones and contribute nothing to this result.
----------------------------------------------------------------------

All input is converted to SI units internally, and the result is reported together with the model's limitations. This is a static, homogeneous-sphere model: it does not simulate gravitational collapse, and it does not predict whether a horizon forms.

Run without a subcommand for the interactive dialogue.

Usage: blackhole [COMMAND]

Commands:
  calc    Compute from any two of mass, physical radius and mean density
  object  Compute for one of the built-in astronomical objects

  help    Print this message or the help of the given subcommand(s)

Options:
  -h, --help
